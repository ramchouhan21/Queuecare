## About This Document

This document defines the primary user personas for QueueCare AI. Each persona is a research-grounded representation of a real user group — built from the stakeholder challenges documented in `02_Problem_Statement.md`, market research findings in `03_Market_Research.md`, and established UX research practices.

Personas are not fictional characters invented for creative purposes. They are composite archetypes derived from documented user behaviors, needs, and pain points observed across Indian hospital outpatient settings. They exist to ensure that every product and design decision is anchored in a genuine understanding of the people who will use QueueCare AI every day.

This document covers four primary personas:

1. [Priya Sharma — The Patient](#persona-1--priya-sharma--the-patient)
2. [Ramesh Kumar — The Receptionist](#persona-2--ramesh-kumar--the-receptionist)

## Persona 1 — Priya Sharma — The Patient

---

### At a Glance

| Attribute | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Persona Name** | Priya Sharma |
| **Role** | Outpatient Hospital Visitor |
| **Age Range** | 28–55 |
| **Location** | Pune, Maharashtra |
| **Occupation** | School teacher |
| **Household** | Married, two children (ages 6 and 10) |
| **Education** | Bachelor's degree |
| **Primary Language** | Marathi and Hindi; basic English |

---

### Background

Priya is a primary school teacher in a semi-urban neighborhood of Pune. She visits a government or mid-tier private hospital OPD about four to six times a year — sometimes for herself, and often as a caregiver accompanying her children or elderly mother-in-law. She is reasonably comfortable with smartphones and uses WhatsApp and payment apps daily, but she is not a power user of complex digital products.

Her hospital visits are rarely planned far in advance. Most are driven by an illness that has persisted for a few days, a follow-up after medication, or a school health requirement for one of her children. She travels to the hospital by auto-rickshaw or bus and cannot afford to take an entire work day off. Every hour she spends waiting is either unpaid leave or time taken away from her family and household responsibilities.

She is not hostile toward the healthcare system — she trusts her doctors and values the care she receives. But she is deeply frustrated by the unpredictability and discomfort of the waiting process. To Priya, waiting is not the problem. Waiting without knowing is the problem.

---

### Technical Skills

| Skill Area | Level |
|-----------|-------|
| Smartphone use (calls, WhatsApp, UPI payments) | Comfortable |
| App downloads and navigation | Moderate — uses 5–8 apps regularly |
| QR code scanning | Has used it for UPI and COVID vaccination |
| English-language digital interfaces | Basic — prefers Hindi or regional language |
| New app adoption | Hesitant without a clear, immediate benefit |

---

### Goals

1. **Know how long she will wait** — Before or shortly after arriving, she wants a realistic estimate of when she will be seen, so she can plan her time at the hospital.
2. **Not miss her turn** — She wants to be alerted before her number is called so she can step away from the crowded waiting room to sit comfortably, get water, or manage her children.
3. **Get in and out as efficiently as possible** — Her goal is a productive visit that ends in time to return to her other responsibilities.
4. **Feel informed, not ignored** — She wants to know that the system is aware of her and will reach her in an orderly, fair way.
5. **Help her dependents through the process** — When accompanying her elderly mother-in-law or a sick child, she wants the experience to be as calm and navigable as possible.

---

### Responsibilities

- Managing her own outpatient care visits, including chronic condition follow-ups
- Acting as the primary caregiver for her two children during their medical appointments
- Assisting her elderly mother-in-law in navigating the hospital — including registering, waiting, and understanding doctor instructions
- Coordinating her hospital visits around her teaching schedule and family obligations
- Communicating with doctors on behalf of family members who cannot advocate for themselves

---

### Pain Points

| # | Pain Point | Impact |
|---|-----------|--------|
| 1 | **No wait-time information available** — She has no way to know when she will be seen. Asking staff yields vague answers ("maybe 30 minutes," "we don't know"). | She ends up standing near the consultation room door for hours out of fear of missing her turn. |
| 2 | **Missed turns when she steps away** — She once left briefly to buy water for her mother-in-law and returned to find her token had been called and skipped. She had to re-register. | She now feels trapped in the waiting area and cannot leave even for basic needs. |
| 3 | **Crowded, uncomfortable waiting areas** — Rows of chairs are often full. Standing for long periods in heat, near sick patients, with a child or elderly relative is physically exhausting. | The waiting experience itself becomes a health hazard — especially for young children and older adults. |
| 4 | **No clarity on what to do or where to go** — At large hospitals, she is unsure whether she is in the right queue, the right department, or the right building. | She relies on asking other patients or passersby, which is unreliable. |
| 5 | **Paper token system is opaque** — Paper tokens give a number but no context. Token 78 means nothing if she doesn't know they are currently on token 42. | She has no reference point and no ability to estimate. |
| 6 | **Feeling like a number, not a person** — The lack of communication, the crowding, and the uncertainty make her feel that the system does not acknowledge her or respect her time. | This erodes her trust in the hospital even when the clinical care she receives is good. |

---

### Needs

- A clear, honest estimate of how long she will wait — even if it is approximate
- A notification on her phone before her turn so she does not have to stand guard at the consultation room door
- Confidence that she will not lose her place if she briefly steps away
- A simple process that does not require her to learn a complicated new interface during a stressful visit
- An experience that works even if she has only basic smartphone skills
- Support for registering a family member — especially an elderly relative who cannot use a smartphone
- Information in a language she is comfortable with (Hindi or regional language)

---

### Motivations

- She is motivated by care for her family. She does not visit hospitals for herself as much as she does for the people who depend on her.
- She values time efficiency deeply — every hour saved in a hospital is an hour returned to her teaching or family life.
- She wants a sense of fairness. She accepts that others may be seen before her, but she wants to know the order is based on a legitimate system, not on who pushes harder or shouts louder.
- She is motivated by reduced anxiety. The physical act of waiting is less difficult when she has information. Uncertainty — not duration — is her primary source of stress.

---

### Frustrations

- Seeing patients who arrived after her somehow get called before her, with no explanation
- Being told to "just wait" repeatedly with no time reference
- Watching the hospital feel chaotic and understaffed during peak hours while no one communicates what is happening
- Having to repeat her personal information at every counter because systems do not share data
- Feeling rushed during the consultation itself — sensing that the doctor is under pressure from the backlog waiting outside
- Carrying her elderly mother-in-law through multiple floors and counters without any guidance on the sequence of steps

---

### Daily Workflow on a Hospital Visit Day

**6:45 AM** — Wakes up early to prepare children for school and arrange for her mother-in-law to be ready.

**8:15 AM** — Leaves home with her mother-in-law. Takes an auto-rickshaw to the hospital. Arrives with no information on current wait status.

**8:45 AM** — Joins the registration queue. Waits 20–35 minutes to receive a paper token from the reception desk. Paper says "Token 67." No other information is provided.

**9:20 AM** — Walks to the orthopedics OPD waiting area. A chalkboard says "Now Serving: Token 38." She has no idea how long 29 tokens will take. She sits near the door, afraid to move.

**9:20 AM – 11:30 AM** — Waits. Gets up periodically to check the display. Answers her mother-in-law's questions. Manages a phone call from her school. Asks a staff member "how long?" and receives a shrug.

**11:35 AM** — Token 67 is called verbally. She barely hears it over the noise. She and her mother-in-law enter the consultation room.

**11:45 AM** — Consultation complete. Doctor prescribes tests and medications. She now needs to find the laboratory counter and pharmacy.

**12:30 PM** — Leaves the hospital. She has spent 3 hours and 45 minutes. The consultation itself was 10 minutes.

---

### How QueueCare AI Helps Priya

QueueCare AI changes Priya's experience from one of passive, anxious waiting to one of informed, comfortable waiting.

She scans a QR code at the OPD entrance and receives a digital token on her phone within seconds. She sees her position (Token 67, position 29 in queue) and an estimated wait range ("approximately 55–75 minutes"). Rather than sitting near the door, she finds a bench outside, buys water, and sits with her mother-in-law.

When 5 patients remain ahead of her, her phone notifies her to return to the waiting area. When she is next in line, she receives another notification. When the doctor calls her token, a final notification sends her directly to the consultation room — calmly, on time, without crowding or confusion.

If her mother-in-law does not have a smartphone, the receptionist can issue her token from the desk. Priya can track it on her own phone on her behalf.

The entire waiting experience is still present — the wait time does not magically disappear — but the experience of waiting is transformed. Priya knows. She can plan. She does not miss her turn. She trusts the system.

---

### User Journey Summary

| Stage | Current Experience | With QueueCare AI |
|-------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Arrival** | Joins an opaque crowd at the registration desk with no information | Scans a QR code or checks in at reception; receives a token and wait estimate immediately |
| **Waiting** | Sits near the consultation room door for hours, afraid to move | Waits comfortably anywhere in or around the hospital with live position on her phone |
| **Turn approaching** | Hears a verbal call, may miss it; cannot predict when to return | Receives a push notification when a few patients remain ahead; returns calmly |
| **Being called** | Verbal announcement over noise; may miss turn and need to re-queue | Receives a direct notification on her phone when her token is called |
| **Consultation** | Rushed consultation; doctor under pressure from visible backlog outside | Orderly entry; doctor's environment is calmer; consultation is not disrupted |
| **Post-visit** | Leaves exhausted and uncertain about the time spent | Leaves having had a predictable, transparent experience she is likely to return to |

## Persona 2 — Ramesh Kumar — The Receptionist

---

### At a Glance

| Attribute | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Persona Name** | Ramesh Kumar |
| **Role** | OPD Receptionist / Front-Desk Registration Staff |
| **Age Range** | 24–40 |
| **Location** | Hyderabad, Telangana |
| **Occupation** | Senior Receptionist, General Medicine OPD |
| **Education** | 12th standard + diploma in hospital administration or BBA |
| **Experience** | 5 years in hospital front-desk operations |
| **Primary Language** | Telugu and Hindi; functional English |

---

### Background

Ramesh works as a senior receptionist at the outpatient registration desk of a 150-bed private hospital in Hyderabad. He has been in this role for five years and is considered one of the more experienced staff members on the floor. He manages the General Medicine OPD counter, which typically handles 80 to 130 patients on a standard morning session between 8:30 AM and 1:00 PM.

He joined the hospital after completing a diploma in hospital administration. Over time, he has become deeply familiar with the rhythms of the OPD — the peak arrival window between 9:00 and 11:00 AM, the patients who need special attention, and the recurring bottlenecks that make certain days harder than others. Despite this experience, he works largely with paper tokens, handwritten registers, and a basic hospital management system that is used primarily for billing, not queue management.

Ramesh is not resistant to technology. He uses WhatsApp extensively and is comfortable with digital tools that are intuitive and fast. His resistance is toward tools that slow him down or require extensive training during an already pressured workday. He is effective at his job because he has learned to work around the system's limitations — but he recognizes the inefficiencies and the toll they take on him and his patients.

---

### Technical Skills

| Skill Area | Level |
|-----------|-------|
| Basic computer and data entry | Comfortable |
| Hospital Management System (HMS) — data entry modules | Intermediate |
| Smartphone and messaging apps | Comfortable |
| Digital dashboards and new software | Moderate — adaptable with a brief orientation |
| Typing speed under pressure | Fast — developed through high-volume registration |

---

### Goals

1. **Register patients as quickly as possible during the peak window** — Between 9:00 and 11:00 AM, speed is everything. Every second saved per patient matters when 30 people are standing in line.
2. **Know the queue status at a glance** — He wants to see what is happening in the queue without having to ask the doctor or walk to the waiting area.
3. **Reduce the number of "how long do I have to wait?" questions** — This is his single biggest time drain. If patients had their own answers, he could focus on registering new arrivals.
4. **Handle priority patients fairly and without conflict** — He needs to flag elderly patients, emergencies, and pregnant women for priority without creating visible resentment among waiting patients.
5. **End the session cleanly** — He wants to close out the day's queue with an accurate record of who was seen, who did not show up, and how long the session ran.

---

### Responsibilities

- Opening and managing the OPD registration counter at the start of each session
- Registering new and returning patients and issuing paper tokens in sequential order
- Answering patient questions about queue status, department locations, and expected wait times
- Coordinating with the doctor's room attendant when patients need to be re-sequenced
- Identifying and flagging patients who need priority handling (elderly, emergency, pediatric)
- Managing the physical crowd at the registration desk during peak-hour surges
- Reconciling paper token records at the end of each session
- Escalating problems — such as a doctor running late or an emergency in the waiting area — to the department supervisor
- Handling patient complaints and de-escalating conflict when patients become frustrated about wait times

---

### Pain Points

| # | Pain Point | Impact |
|---|-----------|--------|
| 1 | **Repetitive status questions consume 30–40% of his time** — Patients constantly ask "how long is the wait?" and "am I in the right queue?" He has no reliable answer to give because he does not know himself. | He cannot focus on registration; the queue slows down; more patients become frustrated. |
| 2 | **Paper tokens are error-prone and hard to manage** — Tokens get lost, duplicated, and disputed. Patients claim they were skipped or that their number was already called while they were in the restroom. | Every dispute requires time to investigate and resolve, often in front of a crowded waiting area. |
| 3 | **No real-time visibility into the doctor's room** — He does not know how many patients have been seen, how quickly the doctor is consulting, or whether the queue is falling behind. | He cannot give patients even approximate answers, and he cannot escalate delays proactively. |
| 4 | **Peak-hour crowd management is physically exhausting** — During 9:00–11:00 AM, the registration desk is surrounded by impatient patients. Maintaining order while entering data and issuing tokens simultaneously requires intense focus. | Staff burnout, errors under pressure, and elevated conflict risk every single morning. |
| 5 | **Priority flagging creates conflict** — When he allows an elderly patient or a pregnant woman to be seen earlier, other patients who have been waiting challenge him. Without a visible, systematic basis for prioritization, it looks like favoritism. | He avoids using priority too freely because of the confrontation it triggers, even when the clinical need is genuine. |
| 6 | **End-of-session reconciliation is manual and unreliable** — Counting paper stubs, cross-referencing names, and identifying no-shows requires 20–30 minutes of manual work at the end of each session. | Data is incomplete and inaccurate; management has no reliable daily statistics. |

---

### Needs

- A fast token issuance interface that is no slower than the current paper process — ideally faster
- The ability to see the live queue status on a screen at his desk without walking to the waiting area
- A way to flag priority patients that is visible to both the doctor and the patient, reducing conflict
- Automated patient notifications so fewer patients approach the desk asking for updates
- A session summary that generates automatically without manual counting
- An interface that works reliably under high-volume, high-stress conditions — no crashes, no slowdowns
- A system that does not require extensive retraining — it should feel intuitive within a day or two
- The ability to cancel, skip, or re-sequence tokens quickly when situations demand it

---

### Motivations

- Ramesh takes pride in running a smooth, orderly registration session. A well-managed morning gives him a sense of professional competence and control.
- He is motivated by reducing the conflict in his workday. Patient frustration directed at him is the most draining part of his job, and he knows most of it stems from the waiting experience, not from anything he has done wrong.
- He wants to be seen as a capable, effective staff member by his supervisors. If a digital tool makes him visibly more efficient, he will embrace it.
- He has been doing this job long enough to recognize that the manual process is not sustainable. Younger patients especially are accustomed to digital experiences and become more impatient with paper-based systems each year.

---

### Frustrations

- Spending the first hour of every session in a state of controlled chaos that could be better managed with basic digital tools
- Bearing the brunt of patient frustration that is rooted in a systemic problem he cannot fix — slow queue progression driven by doctor pace and patient volume that are beyond his control
- Watching patients crowd around his desk not to register but to complain about the wait, while newly arrived patients still need to be registered
- Having no way to know in advance that today will be a heavy day — he has no visibility into expected patient volumes before the session starts
- Paper reconciliation at the end of a long session when he is tired and just wants to leave
- Receiving complaints from the department supervisor about poor queue management when the tools he has been given are inadequate for the volume he handles

---

### Daily Workflow

**8:00 AM** — Arrives at the hospital, opens the registration counter, and prepares the paper token pad, register, and computer system.

**8:30 AM** — First patients arrive. Registration begins. He takes patient name, age, and visit purpose; writes a sequential token number; hands over the paper slip.

**9:00 – 11:00 AM** — Peak hour. The crowd at his desk grows to 10–20 people at once. He registers patients as quickly as possible — one every 60–90 seconds when the flow is smooth. He simultaneously fields status questions from patients waiting behind the crowd.

**Every 10 minutes** — He or a colleague walks to the waiting area to check what token number the doctor is currently serving. He returns and mentally calculates approximate wait times for new arrivals.

**Throughout the morning** — Patients approach his desk repeatedly asking "how long?" He gives an estimate he is not confident in. Some patients leave. Some argue. Some accept the answer and return to the waiting area.

**10:30 AM** — An elderly woman is brought in by her son. He knows he should prioritize her. He verbally tells her to move to the front of the doctor's queue. Three other patients protest loudly. He spends 8 minutes managing the resulting conflict.

**12:45 PM** — Patient intake slows. He begins reconciling: counting paper stubs, crossing off seen patients, identifying gaps (no-shows). He produces a rough count on paper.

**1:30 PM** — Session closes. He files his paper register for the day. No one will likely review the data in any systematic way.

---

### How QueueCare AI Helps Ramesh

QueueCare AI directly addresses every major pain point in Ramesh's workday.

He can register a patient and issue a digital token in under 60 seconds. Once issued, the token appears in the live queue on his dashboard — he no longer needs to walk to the waiting area to check progress. Because patients receive automatic notifications on their phones when their turn approaches, the volume of "how long?" questions drops dramatically.

When he needs to flag an elderly or emergency patient as priority, the system visibly repositions the token in the queue and notifies the doctor. The prioritization is transparent and systematic — not a personal decision made under social pressure. Other patients can see on their own screens that a priority case was inserted, and the reason category is clear.

At end of session, the dashboard shows him a live summary: tokens issued, served, no-shows, and cancelled. No manual counting. The data is already there.

For the first time, Ramesh can focus on what his role is actually supposed to be: processing registrations efficiently and ensuring patients are directed to the right place.

---

### User Journey Summary

| Stage | Current Experience | With QueueCare AI |
|-------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Session open** | Sets up paper token pad, register, and HMS counter manually | Opens the session on the dashboard; system is ready to issue digital tokens |
| **Patient registration** | Writes token, records details in register — 60–90 seconds if smooth, longer if queries | Registers patient in the system; token issued automatically with queue position and wait estimate |
| **Queue monitoring** | Physically walks to the waiting area periodically to check doctor progress | Sees the live queue status on his dashboard — current token, queue depth, status — without leaving his desk |
| **Answering status questions** | Answers 40–50 "how long?" queries per session from patients at the desk | Patients self-track their position on their phones; incoming status queries drop significantly |
| **Priority handling** | Verbal prioritization creates visible conflict with waiting patients | Priority flagging in the system repositions the token transparently; reduces personal confrontation |
| **Session close** | Manual paper reconciliation — 20–30 minutes of counting and estimating | Dashboard session summary is live and accurate; session can be closed with one action |

## Persona 3 — Dr. Anita Desai — The Doctor

---

### At a Glance

| Attribute | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Persona Name** | Dr. Anita Desai |
| **Role** | Senior Physician / OPD Consulting Doctor |
| **Age Range** | 38–55 |
| **Location** | Mumbai, Maharashtra |
| **Specialty** | General Medicine |
| **Qualification** | MBBS, MD (Internal Medicine) |
| **Work Setting** | 200-bed private multi-specialty hospital |
| **Experience** | 15 years post-MD clinical practice |
| **Primary Language** | Hindi and Marathi; professional English |

---

### Background

Dr. Anita Desai is a senior physician who runs an OPD session at a multi-specialty private hospital in Mumbai every weekday morning, from 9:00 AM to 1:00 PM. In a typical session she sees between 30 and 45 patients. She has been practicing at this hospital for eight years and is well-regarded by her patients and peers.

Like most OPD doctors in high-volume settings, Dr. Desai's clinical work is embedded in a broken operational system. The consultation room is her domain — inside it, she is in full control and deeply skilled. But the moment a patient walks through the door, the experience is shaped by a process that happens entirely outside her control: who is next, when they arrive, whether there are urgent cases she needs to see sooner, and how many patients are still waiting.

She is not against technology. She uses smartphone apps and is comfortable with digital records when they are well-designed. But she has seen enough poorly implemented hospital software to be appropriately skeptical of tools that claim to solve operational problems. She will adopt a tool that makes her clinical session better. She will ignore — or actively resist — a tool that adds administrative burden without delivering a meaningful improvement.

---

### Technical Skills

| Skill Area | Level |
|-----------|-------|
| Medical software and digital records (EMR) | Intermediate — proficient in the hospital's HMS |
| Smartphone apps | Comfortable for communication and reference |
| Digital dashboards and workflow tools | Moderate — adapts well to well-designed interfaces |
| Typing and data entry during consultation | Minimal — clinical notes are brief in OPD settings |
| Tolerance for poorly designed software | Low — she will abandon tools that slow her down |

---

### Goals

1. **Run a productive, uninterrupted consultation session** — Her primary goal every morning is to see her patients effectively, without interruptions breaking her clinical focus.
2. **Know who is waiting and in what order** — She wants a clear view of the queue — how many patients remain, who is next, and whether any cases need to be moved earlier.
3. **Identify urgent cases before they walk in the door** — If a patient has been flagged as emergency or high-priority, she wants to know before they enter the room so she can mentally prepare and adjust order if needed.
4. **Call the next patient without leaving the room** — Calling patients currently requires her to open the door and announce, or rely on a room attendant who is not always available. She wants to signal readiness without breaking the consultation workflow.
5. **Finish on time** — She has responsibilities after OPD hours — ward rounds, meetings, and her own family. A session that runs 45 minutes over schedule cascades negatively through her entire day.

---

### Responsibilities

- Conducting outpatient consultations for general medicine patients — diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up
- Assessing patient urgency and determining the appropriate order of consultation when required
- Communicating diagnoses and treatment plans clearly to patients and caregivers
- Prescribing medications, investigations, and referrals
- Managing the consultation room environment — pacing, patient time, and clinical documentation
- Coordinating with reception when a case requires special sequencing (e.g., an elderly patient who cannot wait, or an emergency case)
- Participating in hospital clinical governance meetings and quality reviews

---

### Pain Points

| # | Pain Point | Impact |
|---|-----------|--------|
| 1 | **Patients and attendants knock on the consultation room door** — While she is mid-consultation, people knock to ask when they will be seen, to check if they are in the right queue, or to request priority. | Each interruption breaks clinical focus, unsettles the patient being seen, and lengthens the consultation. |
| 2 | **No visibility into waiting patients without leaving the room** — She has no way to know how many patients are still waiting, which one is next, or whether any are in visible distress in the corridor. | She is clinically blind to everything outside her door. She makes operational decisions based on incomplete information. |
| 3 | **Priority sequencing is informal and inconsistent** — When a priority case needs to be moved up, it is communicated verbally — by the room attendant, by the patient's family member knocking on the door, or by the receptionist calling the room phone. | The process is disruptive and unreliable. Some genuine priority cases are missed. Some non-urgent cases are pushed forward by persistent attendants. |
| 4 | **Pressure from the visible backlog creates clinical risk** — When she can see or sense that a large number of patients are waiting, the psychological pressure to move faster is real. Complex cases that need more time compete with the awareness of patients who have been waiting for hours. | This can subtly shorten consultations, which can compromise care quality. She is aware of this and it adds to her stress. |
| 5 | **No data on her own consultation patterns** — She does not know her average consultation duration per day, how many patients she sees before delays build up, or how her pace compares to the session schedule. | Without data, she cannot self-regulate or advocate for appropriate patient volume limits. |
| 6 | **Coordination with reception requires phone calls or messengers** — If she needs to pause, skip a patient, or re-sequence the queue, she has to make a phone call or send the room attendant out — both of which take time and interrupt the clinical rhythm. | Small operational adjustments become disproportionately disruptive. |

---

### Needs

- A live view of her patient queue — visible from her workstation inside the consultation room, without needing to step outside or make a phone call
- The ability to call the next patient and send them a notification from within her room — a single click that replaces verbal calling or attendant reliance
- Clear visual identification of priority patients in the queue so she can make clinical decisions about sequencing proactively, not reactively
- The ability to adjust patient order when clinical circumstances require it — and to do so quickly without administrative overhead
- A tool that adds zero new documentation requirements. She is already documenting clinical notes; the queue tool must not ask her to enter data she is not already recording
- A reliable system — one that works consistently every morning without crashes or slow loading
- No dependency on an internet connection inside a consultation room that may have intermittent Wi-Fi

---

### Motivations

- Dr. Desai is motivated primarily by her patients. She became a physician to help people, and every disruption to her clinical session is time taken away from the person in front of her.
- She is motivated by professional pride. She wants to run a session that ends on time, serves the right number of patients appropriately, and leaves her feeling that she did her job well.
- She is motivated by reducing the chaotic environment outside her door. She knows the crowding is not her fault, but she experiences it viscerally and wants it to improve.
- She is mildly motivated by data — she would welcome session-level feedback on her consultation pace and patient volumes, if it came without additional effort on her part.

---

### Frustrations

- Attendants who force their way into the consultation room claiming emergency when the situation is not urgent
- Discovering, mid-session, that a patient with a high-urgency condition has been waiting for two hours in the general queue because no one flagged them
- Being told by administration to "see more patients" without any acknowledgment of the operational constraints that limit throughput
- Having no way to signal "I need five minutes" to the waiting area without physically opening the door and shouting
- Receiving patients in the wrong order because the room attendant called the wrong token number
- Ending sessions that are 45–60 minutes over schedule because the intake process at registration did not manage the queue well

---

### Daily Workflow

**8:45 AM** — Arrives at the hospital, reviews overnight ward notes, and prepares for the OPD session. She has no information about how many patients have registered today or whether any have been flagged urgent.

**9:00 AM** — Session begins. Room attendant opens the door and calls "Number 1." The first patient enters.

**9:00 AM – 1:00 PM** — Consultation rhythm: patient enters, she consults, patient exits. Between patients, the attendant calls the next number verbally. Periodically, someone knocks mid-consultation to ask about their turn.

**10:15 AM** — An attendant enters the room to say that a patient outside has been waiting for three hours and is not feeling well. She has no context on this patient — who they are, what they are here for, or how they compare to others in the queue. She instructs the attendant to bring them in next.

**10:20 AM** — Two patients challenge the sequence change outside the door. She hears raised voices through the wall. It unsettles the patient currently in the room.

**12:30 PM** — She is on Token 32 of 41. The session is already running behind. She knows she will finish late.

**1:50 PM** — Last patient exits. Session ran 50 minutes over. She has no data on today's session other than a rough memory of how many patients she saw.

---

### How QueueCare AI Helps Dr. Desai

QueueCare AI gives Dr. Desai something she has never had inside her consultation room: a window into the queue outside her door.

From her workstation, she can see the full queue — patient names, token numbers, position, and any priority flags. When she is ready for the next patient, she clicks one button. The patient receives a notification on their phone and walks in calmly, without an attendant needing to shout down the corridor. No knocking. No confusion.

Priority patients are clearly marked with a color indicator — she can see at a glance if an emergency case is in the queue and adjust the order proactively, before the situation becomes a corridor confrontation. Her manual reorder decisions are logged so the system updates all affected patients automatically.

Because she calls patients herself, she controls the pace. A complex case that needs 20 minutes gets 20 minutes. She does not feel pressured by an invisible crowd she cannot quantify.

Over time, session-level analytics show her average consultation time, patients seen per session, and how she is tracking against the session schedule — data she can use to advocate for appropriate patient volume limits or to adjust her own pacing.

### Background

Suresh is the Operations Manager at a 180-bed multi-specialty private hospital in South Delhi. He has worked in hospital administration for nearly two decades, starting as a floor supervisor and working his way up through roles in billing, patient services, and department coordination. He now oversees the operational performance of the entire facility, including five active OPD departments, over 60 clinical and administrative staff, and the hospital's ongoing reputation for service quality.

His day is not spent in one place. He moves between floors, between departments, and between staff members — troubleshooting, escalating, and preventing the crises that emerge when a hospital of this size runs without adequate coordination. He does this largely without real-time data. His understanding of what is happening in each department on any given morning is based on physical observation, anecdotal reports from staff, and periodic phone calls with department heads.

Suresh is not a technology skeptic. He has successfully championed the adoption of a hospital management system for billing and records, and he understands the value of digital tools. But he has also experienced failed IT implementations — solutions that were oversold, poorly designed for the operational reality of Indian hospitals, and quietly abandoned by staff within weeks of launch. He evaluates new tools on a simple criterion: does it actually make operations better, or does it just look good in a presentation?

### Goals

1. **Maintain consistent, measurable service quality across all OPD departments** — He is accountable to the hospital's medical director and ownership for patient experience and operational performance. Long queue waits are a direct reflection on him.
2. **Identify and resolve bottlenecks before they become complaints** — He wants to know that Department X has a queue of 60 patients at 10:00 AM before the complaint reaches the medical director at 2:00 PM.
3. **Make staffing and resource decisions based on evidence, not instinct** — He knows from experience that certain days and certain departments run heavy, but he has no reliable data to validate this or to justify staff allocation decisions.
4. **Demonstrate measurable improvement over time** — His department supervisors and the hospital ownership want to see that operational investments produce results. He needs data to make that case.
5. **Scale the operational model as the hospital grows** — The hospital is expanding. The manual processes that work (barely) for 5 departments will not work for 8. He needs systems that can grow with the facility.

### Responsibilities

- Overall operational performance of the hospital's outpatient and administrative functions
- Staff planning, scheduling, and performance oversight for reception, ward coordination, and administrative teams
- Monitoring patient experience quality indicators — particularly wait times, complaint volume, and patient feedback
- Coordinating between clinical department heads and administrative staff
- Managing vendor relationships — including HMS providers, facility services, and support contracts
- Reporting to the Medical Director and hospital ownership on operational KPIs
- Leading digital transformation initiatives — evaluating, procuring, and implementing operational tools

### Needs

- Staff account management that he controls — who has access, to which departments, and at what role
- Reports that he can extract and present to the medical director and hospital ownership in management meetings
- A system that staff will actually adopt — intuitive, fast, and visibly better than what they currently do
- Pricing and deployment that is appropriate for a mid-tier private hospital — not enterprise-grade complexity or enterprise-grade cost

### How QueueCare AI Helps Suresh

QueueCare AI gives Suresh something he has never had in 18 years of hospital operations: a single screen that tells him what is happening in every OPD department right now.

## Cross-Persona Insights

The four personas above represent distinct roles, but their experiences of the hospital queue are deeply interconnected. Understanding these connections is as important as understanding each persona individually.

---

### The Chain of Dysfunction

The hospital queue is a system, and its dysfunction propagates across all four personas in a chain:

1. **Priya** arrives without information and waits, anxious and uncertain. She asks Ramesh repeatedly how long she will wait.
2. **Ramesh** cannot answer because he does not know — he has no visibility into the doctor's room. He absorbs 40–50 status questions per session and cannot focus on registration.
3. **Dr. Desai** has no view of the waiting room outside her door. Patients and attendants knock during consultations. She cannot signal readiness without relying on an attendant. She finishes late.
4. **Suresh** discovers all of this after the fact — through a patient complaint, a staff escalation, or a floor walk that gives him a 45-minute-old snapshot.

When QueueCare AI improves any one of these links, the benefit cascades through the others. When Priya can track her position on her phone, Ramesh answers fewer questions. When Ramesh can see the live queue, he can give better information. When Dr. Desai can call patients herself, interruptions drop. When Suresh can see all of this in real time, he can intervene before it breaks.

| Need | Priya | Ramesh | Dr. Desai | Suresh |
|------|-------|--------|-----------|--------|
| Real-time queue information | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Reduced uncertainty | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | — |
| Less manual effort | — | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Transparency and fairness | ✓ | ✓ | — | — |
| Operational data and visibility | — | — | — | ✓ |
| Simple, fast interaction | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

### Design Tensions to Watch

| Tension | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Speed vs. completeness** | Ramesh needs to register patients in seconds. Collecting more data per patient — for better analytics — cannot slow the registration process during peak hour. |
| **Transparency vs. anxiety** | Priya needs enough information to plan her time. Too much information (e.g., "the doctor is currently 80 minutes behind schedule") may increase anxiety rather than reduce it. |
| **Doctor control vs. system automation** | Dr. Desai values control over her session. The system must support her decisions — not override them. Automation must feel like a tool she wields, not a constraint imposed on her. |
| **Simplicity vs. depth** | Suresh needs deep analytics to manage the hospital. Ramesh needs a simple interface to manage the queue. These are the same system, and both must be true simultaneously. |
